# Research log: Where to Build Next

## 20-21 October 2026: brief to submission

**The brief.** DSN's "Mapping Nigeria's Digital Divide Through Geospatial Data": integrate at least two external datasets with the supplied Ookla tiles, find underserved communities relative to population, settlement and socioeconomic need, recommend priorities. Deliverables: interactive map, at least three insights with recommendations, documentation. Submission: a PDF of at most two pages. Deadline 21 October 2026.

**Choosing the problem.** Four directions were weighed against the six scoring criteria (core ranking plus clinics, plus schools, core only, data deserts). Oluwasola asked for a real problem to be solved rather than a map to be drawn, so the project became a siting tool: where should the next connectivity sites go? Candidate sites are registered health facilities, which the National Broadband Plan 2020-2025 itself targets (connect 80% of primary health centres per LGA, p. 40).

**Data facts established.**
- DSN's GeoPackage holds two identical layers; one lacks a CRS. It states neither type nor period. Matching the eight busiest tiles against Ookla's public Parquet files on AWS identified the release exactly: mobile, Q4 2024 (8 of 8 identical on every field). Ookla has published seven newer quarters since.
- The speed benchmark was verified in the plan's own text: broadband is at least 25 Mbps urban and 10 Mbps rural by 2025 (pp. 36, 38).
- References were checked at source: Church and ReVelle (1974), Nemhauser, Wolsey and Fisher (1978), Chi et al. (2022), the GHSL citation from GHSL's own data package, and Ookla's licence and suggested attribution from its README.
- The downloaded wealth file is 3,476,045 bytes against 3,477,572 listed on HDX; the checksum in PROVENANCE.csv records what was used.

**A design flaw found in self-review.** The first run treated every gap as a coverage gap. Its first sites were in towns (Okene, 642,000 "reached"), which no single site can serve: in towns the problem is capacity. The design changed to a four-way triage (served, slow, unmeasured town, rural coverage gap) with siting on rural coverage gaps only. This is more realistic and became the project's central idea: not every gap needs a tower.

**A data artefact found in self-review.** Site 1 was reaching 153,000 people because one 1 km cell held 126,531 people in an area GHSL calls low-density rural. Meta's CSV has 256 pixels carrying one of two repeated values (2,433.3 and 1,603.1 people per ~31 m pixel; 545,717 people) in Yamaltu/Deba (Gombe), Isokan (Osun) and Ona-Ara (Oyo). No genuine pixel exceeds 333 (99.999th percentile). Pixels above 1,000 are excluded as unplaceable; the population falls from 206.14 M to 205.60 M. The same Meta layer feeds Health Under Fire, so its figures for those three LGAs carry the artefact; national and state totals are barely affected.

**Palette.** The four classes were checked with the dataviz validator: grey for "measure first" failed against the other colours, so it became blue. The final palette passes every check on all pairs (worst colour-blind separation 8.9, normal vision 20.2); amber is below 3:1 on white, so every class is also labelled.

**Results (run of 21 October 2026).** 53.0 M served, 22.1 M slow, 64.6 M unmeasured town, 66.0 M rural coverage gap; 80% of the gap is in the poorer 40%. 100 sites reach 3.04 M (1.66 to 7.88 M across site reach of 3 to 10 km). Poorest first reaches 458,000 more poorer people for 275,000 fewer overall.

**Integrity check.** 17 of 17 checks pass, including a great-circle recomputation of 3,000 random cells (100% agreement) and brute-force coverage of the chosen sites (within 0.4%). The PDF build fails if it exceeds two pages.

**Ideas for the research.** The efficiency-equity trade-off in connectivity siting is a publishable question; extend with newer Ookla quarters, fixed broadband, capacity constraints in towns, backhaul distance, and validation against operator coverage where obtainable.

**Judge-style self-review, final pass.** The brief asks which communities need connectivity; the entry answered which clinics. LGA rankings now appear in the PDF (top five per need) and as a "Communities by need" tab in the tool, led by the brief's own three criteria together: rural, poorer 40%, no broadband evidence (Ningi, Fune, Wasagu/Danko, Ganjuwa, Gamawa). Recommendations now name who acts (the Universal Service Provision Fund, operators, the Nigerian Communications Commission, NPHCDA and state primary health care boards). Testing the tab found two bugs, both fixed: a CSS rule that kept the hidden list visible, and a share that described a different group from the number beside it. The integrity check now also verifies the LGA tables: 21 of 21 pass.

**Schools added as hosts (21 October 2026).** At Oluwasola's request, schools join clinics as candidate hosts: GRID3 NGA Education Facilities (2020 data, CC BY), 204,513 schools, of which 204,355 fall inside an LGA (72% public, 27% private; 76% primary, 22% secondary, 0.9% tertiary). Oluwasola chose to keep public and private hosts. Candidates are thinned to one per 1 km cell (clinic first), since coverage is measured on that grid; clinic-only reach at 100 sites moved by 382 people as a result. JAMB CBT centres were also requested but left out: JAMB publishes no official list with locations, only unofficial address lists, and CBT centres need internet already, so they sit mostly in connected towns. The rewrite exposed a bug in the poorest-first run, which counted only poorer people as reached; coverage now spans every gap cell while poverty only weights the choice, and a guard stops the build if the bug returns. With both hosts, 100 sites reach 3.12 M people (3.04 M with clinics alone), 47 of them at schools, and 87 of the 100 chosen squares hold two or more possible hosts. Integrity check: 23 of 23.

**Self-contained repository and a new tool layout (21 October 2026).** The project used to read boundaries, the cleaned clinic list and the population file from the Health Under Fire folder, so the public code could not run on its own. The boundaries, the cleaned GRID3 clinic list and DSN's Ookla tiles (24 MB together) now ship in `data/input/`. The population CSV (152 MB) is fetched from HDX by step 01; it is byte-for-byte identical to DSN's copy (same SHA-256). Step 01 now checks every download against `data/raw/PROVENANCE.csv` and stops on a mismatch. A full rebuild from step 03 gave the same headline numbers, and 23 of 23 checks pass. With both hosts, poorest first reaches 397,713 more poorer people for 250,790 fewer overall at 100 sites. The tool was rebuilt around the map: a fixed control panel on the left, a map that fills the rest of the window, the legend on the map itself, and a single-column layout on phones. It now has a viewport tag, which the earlier version lacked, so it no longer renders at desktop width on a phone.

**Judge-style review and fixes (21 October 2026, before submission).** A strict read of the PDF against the rubric found two things a judge could catch on the page itself. First, only 1 of the 100 sites fell in the five priority LGAs, while Action 1 implied they overlap. The reason was checked before it was written down: those LGAs hold 126 to 210 poorer people per km² of gap, against 482 under the 100 chosen sites, and in four of the five the best possible single site reaches fewer poorer people than the 100th site chosen (Gamawa is the exception, and it holds the one site). The PDF now says so, and Action 1 asks for a budget line of their own. Second, the PDF recommended choosing the poorest first but showed the other run's top ten; the table and the map now show the poorest-first sites, and the tool opens on that run. "Rural coverage gap" became "Rural gap: no test within 2 km", since nothing here confirms the absence of a signal, and Limitations now says gaps come from where people test, not from operator maps or mast locations. The speed data itself is now used: where people test, the average download is 52.2 Mbps in towns and 22.9 Mbps in rural areas (weighted by tests; latency 56 and 54 ms), only 628 of 9,234 tiles are rural, and only 38.9% of town tiles reach 25 Mbps. The map gained grey state borders (white ones vanished on unpopulated areas) and nine cities, each checked against its OCHA state polygon. Every new number is recomputed in step 08 by another route (raw file and re-sampled labels for speeds; every raw host inside each LGA polygon with great-circle distances for the LGA figures). Integrity check: 36 of 36.

**All-year check and a satellite layer (21 October 2026).** A rural gap rests on one quarter of tests, so step 02b fetched Ookla's mobile tiles for January to September 2024 from the public bucket, reading only quadkeys starting 1222 (Nigeria lies inside that zoom-4 tile) and keeping tiles that touch Nigeria. That rule reproduces DSN's October-December selection to within 0.1% (3 extra, 6 missing: DSN used a slightly different border), and all 9,234 of DSN's tiles are identical to the public Q4 file, a stronger confirmation than the 8 sampled tiles in step 02. Result: 95.9% of people in rural gaps (63.3 M of 66.0 M) had no test within 2 km in any quarter of 2024; 97.9% of the poorer 40% in gaps, and 98.7% of the people the first 100 poorest-first sites reach. One quarter alone left 98.2 to 98.5% untested, so pooling the year changes little: the gaps are persistent, not chance. The needs themselves still use Q4 only, as supplied. Step 08 recomputes the share by great-circle distances for 6,000 random gap cells (95.1%, within the sample tolerance). The tool gained a satellite layer, off by default: EOxCloudless 2023 (Sentinel-2, 10 m) by EOX, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 like the Ookla data, with EOX's required credit shown when on. Esri World Imagery was considered and rejected because its terms for use without an account are unclear. Each site's popup has a button that turns the imagery on and zooms to the site, and the needs layer fades close in, since its 1 km squares would hide the ground. Testing found two bugs, both fixed: Leaflet's flyTo fails when the map is already centred on the target (it divides by the distance), and Leaflet redraws popup contents, dropping per-button click handlers. Integrity check: 38 of 38.
